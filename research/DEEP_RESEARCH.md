## PART 3 — THE SEVEN QUESTIONS

### 1. Hair loss as a category

**Recommendation: add it as a distinct NovaHair concern/category, but launch it as “thinning / scalp / stronger-looking hair,” not as a medical hair-regrowth department. Keep drugs and quasi-drug claims out unless you deliberately build a regulated pathway.**

The benchmark breaks into three visibly different models:

1. **Cosmetic / DTC scalp and thinning brands** — VEGAMOUR, Divi, Act+Acre, Scandinavian Biolabs, BondiBoost, Hairlust, Watermans, Pump. Typical one-time hero tickets cluster around roughly **US$25–90 / €30–80 / £15–30 / A$35–70**. Their commercial playbook is quizzes, systems, bundles, before/after, subscription and “scalp environment / stronger-looking / less breakage / fuller-looking” positioning.
2. **Supplement brands** — Nutrafol and JSHealth. These add ingestibles, longer-duration routines and subscriptions. They create more regulatory complexity than topical cosmetics.
3. **Telehealth / medicinal brands** — Hims, Hers, Keeps, Ro, Happy Head, Musely and parts of MDhair. They sell minoxidil, finasteride or compounded prescriptions. This is **not** the model NovaHair should imitate operationally.

For NovaHair, the attractive non-drug assortment is therefore:
- scalp serum or cosmetic strengthening serum;
- gentle shampoo/conditioner for thinning-looking hair;
- scalp massage brush;
- fibre/root camouflage;
- satin/silk or low-friction accessories;
- a routine bundle with a realistic 8–12 week replenishment cycle.

Do **not** use phrases such as “regrows hair,” “treats alopecia,” “stops hair loss,” “prevents baldness,” or drug-adjacent medical language unless the exact product and claim have the required classification/authorisation. Israel’s Ministry of Health explicitly says medicinal or health qualities may not be attributed to cosmetics unless the product is a “dedicated cosmetic” and the quality is directly tied to its approved purpose. The Ministry also maintains a public database for checking licensed/notified cosmetics.  
Sources:  
- https://www.gov.il/en/service/complaint_mislead_medical_advertisement  
- https://www.gov.il/en/service/cosmetics-health-data  
- https://www.gov.il/en/service/cosmetics-marketing  
- https://www.gov.il/en/service/cosmetics-marketing-europe

For commercial cosmetic marketing/import, the current Israeli notification regime requires the relevant dealer/importer registration, a responsible representative, and a product dossier; the European route is available to qualifying products/importers. The brief states that the current China parcels are structured as personal imports by each customer. **I did not find a primary-source page that validates the proposition that this structure automatically removes every Israeli marketing/compliance obligation for the overseas seller. ### 2. Category architecture: one Sephora-style store or two brands/two stores?

The historical data strongly says that **hair and facial skincare behaved like two separate customer missions**: 1,418 skin-only orders, 1,192 hair-only orders, zero mixed orders, and only 37 customers who ever bought across both sides. At the same time, the absolute customer base is small enough that this cannot be treated as an immutable law.

#### Case for one store

There are good global precedents for hair + skin under one roof: Fenty Beauty, Aveda, L’Occitane, Rituals, The Body Shop, Lush, NUXE, Typology, Freshly Cosmetics, Vichy, Ducray, Uriage, Sebamed and others. They make it work by:
- putting **Hair** and **Skin** in clearly separate top-level paths;
- merchandising by concern and routine rather than one giant catalogue;
- using quizzes/diagnostics to route people quickly;
- cross-selling only where the customer intent makes sense;
- retaining one account, one logistics layer and one CRM.

For a 2,663-person list, operating two totally independent technology stacks would duplicate overhead and fragment already-small audiences.

#### Case for two stores / two lists

Your own data is unusually clean. The store had **zero mixed hair+skin baskets** in two years. That is stronger evidence than “customers clicked different categories.” The existing brand names already give you a natural split: NovaHair and NovaGlow. A single mixed homepage risks making a proven hair-colour acquisition visitor wonder why she is looking at collagen masks and facial serum.

A totally separate email calendar is also defensible because the purchase cycles differ:
- colour/root maintenance is event/replenishment driven;
- thinning/scalp care is routine driven;
- masks/serums are skincare-routine driven.

#### My choice

**One Shopify backend/store, two strongly separated storefront journeys and CRM audiences. Not two independent stores yet.**

I would implement:
- Top nav: `NOVAHAIR | NOVAGLOW | מבצעים | מועדון הראשונות`
- NovaHair landing/home: colour, care, thinning/scalp, tools.
- NovaGlow landing/home: masks, serums, hydration, concern-based skincare.
- Search can span both, but PDP recommendations stay within the same mission by default.
- Separate acquisition landing pages, separate post-purchase flows, separate replenishment calendars.
- Cross-sell only after behavioural evidence, not because the catalog allows it.
- “The First Ones” can be one umbrella loyalty identity, but benefits and recommendations should be category-personalised.

Why not two stores now? With only 2,663 subscribers and 324 repeat purchasers, you do not have enough scale to justify splitting reviews, SEO authority, accounts, tracking, support and operations. The observed non-overlap is real evidence, but the sample is still too small to make a permanent corporate separation decision. Treat it as **a routing rule to test**, not a law of nature.

A useful experiment: for 60–90 days expose only 10–15% of repeat purchasers to a tasteful “other universe” cross-sell and measure attach rate. If it remains near zero after sufficient exposure, then a full brand/site separation becomes much easier to justify.

### 4. The September 14-day daily promotion

The weak point is **not simply that 14 emails in 14 days is always wrong**. The weak point is that a small list is being trained to expect a new discount every day while the business has limited behavioural data and no stable delivery experience.

The brief says there are 14 segmented campaigns against a 10,000/month Shopify Messaging allowance. If every campaign were sent to the full 2,663-person list, 14 sends would require **37,282 deliveries**, so segmentation is essential. More importantly, frequency must be controlled at the individual level.

#### What is structurally wrong

- **Too many offer changes.** A different deal every day destroys learning. You cannot tell whether product, creative, discount, segment, day-of-week or novelty caused the result.
- **Discount habituation.** Customers learn that tomorrow may be cheaper.
- **Operational mismatch.** You are increasing purchase pressure while delivery uncertainty is the largest pain point.
- **New-SKU overexposure.** Fifty-six products have zero sales history. A daily calendar can turn the store into a clearance feed before you know what deserves to be a hero.
- **Insufficient control groups.** Without holdouts, incremental revenue is unknowable.
- **CRM collisions.** Daily campaigns can overlap replenishment, abandoned checkout and post-purchase messages.

#### What better operators do

They combine:
- automated lifecycle flows;
- 1–3 campaign sends per week for ordinary engaged customers;
- temporary higher frequency during a real event, with engagement/frequency suppression;
- send-time optimisation;
- category segmentation;
- global suppression for recent purchasers and fatigued users;
- clear unsubscribe / preference centre;
- resend to non-openers only when justified, not a second blast to everyone.

I would change the current 14-day programme to **four campaign moments**, with the landing page still rotating daily if you enjoy the merchandising concept:
1. launch / “September edit”;
2. hair-colour + root-maintenance feature;
3. proven-winners / customer-favourites feature;
4. final 48-hour close.

Between those, let automated flows and retargeting do the work.

**Individual-level cap:** ordinarily no more than ~2 campaign emails in 7 days for an engaged non-buyer, excluding transactional messages; allow 3 during a short event only for recently engaged subscribers. Suppress people who purchased in the last few days from generic promotional sends unless the new message is explicitly post-purchase relevant.

For “The First Ones,” early access is more valuable than another blanket discount. That is consistent with the loyalty patterns found in Kitsch, Freshly, The Body Shop, Aveda, Georgiemane and Fenty-style account benefits: access, shipping, gifts and replenishment are durable; an endless points economy is not mandatory.

### 5. Resurrecting old winners vs pushing new NovaHair concepts

**Do both, but asymmetrically: use old winners to finance and de-risk the restart; use NovaHair concepts to discover the next growth engine.**

The historical evidence for Elastic Dream is much stronger than the evidence for any of the 56 new CJ SKUs:

That does not mean “go back to 2025 exactly.” The business collapsed after its peak, 23% of historical units point to deleted products, and customer expectations/creative/funnel economics may have changed. But it does mean **you should not force the restart to be a pure-new-product experiment**.

My October testing portfolio:
- **40% of merchandising/CRM test capacity: proven historical winners** — Elastic Dream first, then amla if supply/economics are sound.
- **40%: current NovaHair acquisition engine** — colour/root-maintenance creative and offers, because this is the category currently bringing traffic and the CJ colour shelf is exhausted.
- **20%: new concepts** — thinning/scalp, accessories, and selective skin products.

For Elastic Dream specifically:

- use past-customer segmentation (“you bought this before”) and lapsed-customer winback;

- run it against a holdout to measure true resurrection.

For the biotin spray: treat it as a **re-test**, not a proven winner unless the historical order data confirms meaningful sales. “Finished product page” is not product-market fit.

Comparable consumer brands repeatedly lean on hero products while expanding around them. The benchmark dataset shows this pattern in Olaplex No.3, K18 mask, OUAI Leave-In, Fenty hero lines, Kitsch heatless products, BondiBoost routines and Georgiemane’s 10-minute mask. The lesson is not “never launch new products”; it is “newness sits around a small number of heroes.”

### 6. Australia

**Best chance: do not enter Australia as another generic “growth shampoo” brand. Enter with the strongest differentiated NovaHair job-to-be-done — at-home root/grey maintenance and colour-care — plus non-electrical maintenance accessories.**

Australia already has strong hair-growth / scalp incumbents such as BondiBoost and Georgiemane. Their sites show mature routine builders, bundles, rewards, before/after and strongly merchandised hair-growth/repair propositions. Competing head-on with “growth shampoo” gives you no structural advantage.

Your more differentiated opportunity is:
- root/grey maintenance;
- temporary/root camouflage;
- colour-depositing care;
- colour-maintenance masks/conditioners;
- application kits;
- anti-frizz / UV / hydration support positioned cosmetically;
- summer travel/minis and post-swim colour-care.

This is seasonally sensible for Australian spring/summer because colour fade, sun, humidity, pools and frequent washing are intuitive cosmetic problems. Do not overstate a medical effect.

#### Important Australian regulatory nuance

Australia is not simply “send cosmetics and forget regulation.”

AICIS says cosmetics include shampoo, conditioner and hair dye, and a business importing cosmetics into Australia for commercial purposes generally must register with AICIS; there is **no low-value or low-volume threshold** for an Australian commercial importer. Every relevant industrial chemical must also be authorised under an appropriate introduction category.

However, AICIS also gives an important cross-border example: if an **overseas online business sells a cosmetic directly to an Australian customer for that customer’s personal use**, the Australian customer does not need to register with AICIS for that personal-use purchase. That is not the same as saying the overseas seller has no obligations under any Australian law, and it does not cover therapeutic goods.

The TGA is stricter on claims: “promote hair growth” or “decrease/reduce hair loss/alopecia” is specifically given as an example likely to make a hair product therapeutic. Once you cross into therapeutic claims, the cosmetic playbook no longer applies.

Sources:
- https://www.industrialchemicals.gov.au/cosmetics-and-soap/personal-care-skincare-make-and-other-cosmetic-products
- https://www.industrialchemicals.gov.au/cosmetics-and-soap
- https://www.tga.gov.au/resources/guidance/determining-if-your-product-cosmetic-or-therapeutic-good

**Australia launch sequence I would use:**
1. AU-specific landing page in AUD.
2. Three products only: root/grey colour hero, colour-maintenance product, colour-application accessory.
3. Promise only what your supplier tracking can support.
4. No “hair growth / stop hair loss” claims in the Australian test.
5. Run a small paid-social geo test before building an AU-wide catalogue.
6. Only add scalp/thinning cosmetics after a claims and ingredient review.

#### What the Israel reform changes

Israel’s current notification system makes EU-aligned cosmetics easier to commercialise than the legacy case-by-case licensing model, but it does **not** make private-label launch “free” or instant. The Ministry’s basic notification route requires a responsible representative and cosmetic product dossier; the European route is for qualifying importers/products. For a new owned hair-colour line, the realistic workstreams are:
- formula/shade selection and samples;
- safety/ingredient review;
- packaging and Hebrew labelling;
- responsible-representative / dossier work;

A single “one SKU” MOQ is misleading because hair colour is a **shade matrix**. Six shades at even a modest per-shade MOQ multiplies the cash need. That is why I would not give you a fake “$X and 6 weeks” number without manufacturer RFQs.

**Planning range, not quote:** for management purposes I would reserve **several months, not several weeks**, from formula/sample brief to saleable multi-shade inventory, and assume the cash requirement is at least **low five figures USD** once multiple shades, packaging, compliance work and working inventory are included. This is deliberately a planning envelope, not a verified supplier price. It can be materially lower if you choose an existing stock formula/pack and small MOQ; materially higher if you require custom formulation, exclusivity or large production.

#### Capital-allocation decision

Today, own colour is **not** the highest-leverage investment.

You already have evidence that:
- the category pulls traffic;
- supplier colour depth is exhausted;
- customers suffer from delivery uncertainty;
- the store is only beginning to recover.

The best sequence is:

**Stage 1 — 30–45 days:** obtain a faster-stock colour/root solution (EU/US/local), improve tracking and delivery communication, and validate contribution margin at meaningful volume.

**Stage 2 — parallel RFQ:** get actual quotes from 2–3 Israeli/EU manufacturers for 4–6 core shades.

**Stage 3 — decision gate:** private label only after you can show repeatable demand by shade, acceptable return/refund rate, and enough monthly volume that local inventory materially improves economics and CX.

Private label should be the reward for validated demand, not the way you discover whether demand exists.

## Bottom line

NovaHair/NovaGlow does not need another giant import wave. It needs a clearer product hierarchy, faster/more observable fulfilment, and disciplined use of the customers/products that have already proven themselves.
